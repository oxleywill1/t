Clearing a property when you live interstate

Big cleanouts · 8 minute read

A large share of estate and property clearances are organised by someone who lives in another state — often an adult child dealing with a parent's home. It's entirely manageable remotely, but the sequence matters and a few things genuinely require someone physically present.

First, secure and insure

Before anything else: confirm the property is locked, keys are accounted for, and the insurer knows the property is unoccupied. Most home insurance policies have vacancy clauses that reduce or void cover after a set period of the property being empty — often 60 to 90 days. An uninsured empty house is a serious exposure, and this is the most common oversight in remote clearances.

Arrange for someone to check the property periodically if possible — a neighbour, a local family member, or a property manager. Mail piling up visibly signals an empty house.

What you can do remotely

  • Redirect mail through Australia Post
  • Notify utilities, but keep power and water connected until the clearance is done — crews need lighting and often water
  • Obtain quotes from clearance operators using photos or a video walk-through
  • Arrange valuations, with auction houses and dealers assessing from photographs for an initial view
  • Book the clearance, cleaning and any locksmith work

What genuinely needs someone there

The document and valuables search. This is the part that can't be delegated to a removal crew, and it's the part people regret rushing. Wills, deeds, insurance policies, share certificates, jewellery, cash and photographs turn up in unlikely places, and once a house is cleared they're gone.

If you can't travel, the alternatives are a trusted family member, the estate's solicitor, or a specialist clearance operator who offers a documented search-and-set-aside service. Be explicit about what you want retained, and ask for photographs of anything found before it's moved.

Making a remote clearance work

  1. Get a video walk-through before quoting. Ask whoever is on site to walk slowly through every room, the garage, the shed and under the house.
  2. Ask for a written fixed quote based on that footage, with what's included spelled out.
  3. Specify what stays. Send a written list and, ideally, mark items physically with tape or notes.
  4. Request before-and-after photographs as standard, room by room.
  5. Ask for a disposal receipt — useful for estate records where there are multiple beneficiaries.
  6. Arrange access deliberately — a key with a neighbour, a lockbox, or a real estate agent holding keys.

Sequencing with the sale

If the property is being sold, the order that works is: document and valuables search, then clearance, then cleaning, then any repairs or gardening, then photography, then listing. Photographing a partly cleared house is a common and costly mistake, since those images carry the entire campaign.

Where multiple beneficiaries are involved

Remote clearances create the most disputes when other family members feel excluded from decisions. A shared photo album of the house before clearing, a clear list of what's being kept and by whom, and an agreed deadline for anyone to claim items usually prevents the problem entirely. It costs an afternoon and saves relationships.

What happens to the furniture you donate

Disposing of things · 6 minute read

Donating feels better than disposing, and when it works it genuinely is better. But a substantial share of what's donated to Australian charities can't be sold and ends up in landfill — at the charity's expense, funded by money that was meant for their actual work.

The journey of a donation

Furniture arrives at a store or collection point and is assessed. Items in saleable condition are cleaned, tested if electrical, priced and put on the floor. Items that fail assessment are separated for disposal, and the charity pays the tip fees.

Some larger organisations also run repair and refurbishment programs, and some divert material to social enterprises that train people in restoration. Those pathways only apply to items that are structurally sound to begin with.

Why so much is rejected

The common failure points are predictable: fabric tears and stains, water damage, missing fittings on flat-pack items, smoke or pet odour that can't be removed, and safety issues on electrical goods. Upholstered furniture in some states must carry compliant fire labelling to be resold, and items without it can't be accepted regardless of condition.

Used mattresses are refused almost universally, as are cots, car seats and prams, because of safety standards and the impossibility of verifying history.

The after-hours dumping problem

Leaving items outside a closed charity store is illegal dumping. It's also uniquely destructive: overnight the material gets rained on, picked through and scattered, so items that might have sold are ruined by morning. Charities collectively spend a very large amount each year clearing what was left at their doors, and that cost comes directly out of their programs.

How to donate well

  • Phone ahead and describe the item honestly, including any flaws.
  • Deliver during opening hours, or book a collection where offered.
  • Clean it first. A wiped-down, vacuumed item is far more likely to be accepted and sold.
  • Include all fittings, keys, remotes, manuals and cables.
  • Don't disassemble flat-pack furniture unless you're certain it can be rebuilt.
  • Apply the honest test: would you take this into your own home if you had nothing?

Alternatives when charities can't take it

Community giveaway groups and buy-nothing networks move usable-but-imperfect items quickly, and the recipient can see exactly what they're getting. Men's sheds and community workshops sometimes take timber furniture for repair projects. Animal shelters occasionally accept old towels, blankets and linen that no charity shop would sell.

Where nothing applies, disposal is the honest answer — and it's better done by you than passed to a charity that has to pay for it.
